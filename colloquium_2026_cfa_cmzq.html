Star formation drives the evolution of the universe

NASA/WMAP

The backdrop is that star formation drives the evolution of the universe. We had a couple thousand years at the early times where it was just a mixed hot plasma. After that, gravity started to take over, form galaxies, and within those galaxies convert primordial materials into stars and, through the stars, metals. Almost everything we see over cosmic history is star formation, with a little bit of salt sprinkled in in the form of galactic nuclei.

  • After the big bang, there was a brief dark period.
  • After the dark ages, almost all of the light we see comes from star formation in galaxies.
  • Galaxies are made of stars, the observable universe is made of galaxies

I want to break it down a little further. The low-mass stars, anything below about eight solar masses, are dominant by number and by mass. But they produce almost none of the light when the other ones are around: about 1% of the luminosity. On the opposite end, the blue shading, you have the 20 solar mass plus stars. These are the massive OB stars. They produce 95% of the light when they're around, but there's only 1 in 1,000 by number. They're not negligible by either mass or fraction of supernovae, but neither are they dominant. And then this middle group, 8 to 20 solar masses, don't produce that much light. They don't account for that much by number. They're kind of the same-ish bin in mass, but they're actually dominant for supernovae. The supernovae are coming from this relatively narrow mass range, because by number these are a lot greater than the more massive ones.

Cartoon of isolated low-mass star formation

original cartoon by Cormac Purcell, modified by AG
Missing: Neighbors, environment

The cartoon we get from this picture of isolated low-mass star formation is that you have a molecular cloud, and it's a little bit clumpy. In it, there will be cores, regions of overdensity, that will collapse under their own gravity. As they collapse, they will form stars in the center, and those stars will start to produce feedback. At the same time, there will be a disk. Disks are unavoidable. And eventually, the feedback from that central star is going to further heat its core and blow away part of it in an outflow, and then eventually consume the rest. We end up with a star system with a disk around it, and eventually a bare star with maybe some planets hanging around.

What's missing in this picture is any concept of neighbors or a broader environment. And that is the problem.

The cartoon is not universal
Most stars form in crowds, near massive stars.

Guarcello+ 2025: JWST EWOCS Wd1

The cartoon picture isn't universal. Most stars form in regions that are more crowded than today's solar neighborhood. Most of them are forming near massive stars, certainly nearer than our simple picture would allow. Forming stars care about the neighbors and the environment. This picture is from the Westerlund 1 cluster, one of the most massive clusters in our galaxy (shout out to the EWOCS team members in the room). It represents one extreme: only about 5 to 10% of stars in our galaxy today form in an environment as extreme as this. But it's not a negligible population, and their conditions are quite different.

The GC is special on the sky but cosmically commonplace

These crowded conditions are commonplace across the universe. Take this very popular plot from Madau and Dickinson that shows the star formation density per unit time. And that star formation peaked around 10 gigayears ago, around what we call cosmic noon. In this period, stars were forming in smaller and denser and more turbulent volumes. Galaxies were smaller, gas densities were generally higher, and they were more turbulent.

This points us to the Galactic Center: the Galactic Center is special on our sky, but its environmental conditions are commonplace in a cosmic perspective focused on where typical stars formed.

Why the Galactic Center matters beyond the Milky Way -- most stars in the universe formed at cosmic noon, z ~ 1-3 (half before z = 1.3), and they formed in denser gas, in more compact regions -- like the CMZ (Kruijssen & Longmore 2013: same gas surface densities, 1e2-1e3 Msun/pc^2, Mach numbers and gas-to-star ratios). Caveats they list themselves: the CMZ is more metal-rich, high-z galaxies have far more radiation and cosmic rays, and the CMZ forms stars 1-2 dex below the scaling relations. Dozens of questions about the Galactic Center have been open for decades; here are 19, each dated by the paper that posed it (or made it pressing). The OB-star one is the obvious one after the local census (#topdown-local: ~50 O stars within 1.25 kpc of the Sun): nobody knows the CMZ's number. Question 3: (How) does gas reach Sgr A*? Open since 1996. Where we are: candidate pipes in ACES; their 3D positions are disputed. What comes next: 3D positions (JWST, Roman) + an ACES inflow census + sub-pc simulations.

What gas reaches Sgr A*?

Sormani & Barnes 2019; Hatchfield+ 2021; Baganoff+ 2003; Marrone+ 2007; simulations: Tress+ 2024, Feng+ 2026

We have measured bounds on the amount of gas coming in to the CMZ and ending in Sgr A*. We have only models in between. The bar lanes supply a solar mass per year into the Central Molecular Zone. Faraday rotation measurements from SMA and ALMA bound the final accretion flow. Out at the Bondi radius, 10,000 AU, X-ray observations give limits. Those set our boundary conditions. Between those, we have six orders of magnitude where we don't know exactly what's going on. Magnetic fields and star-formation driven feedback are likely the main drivers based on simulations. The open question remains: how much gas reaches Sgr A*, or what feeds our black hole?

About a solar mass per year comes down the bar lanes into the CMZ (Sormani & Barnes 2019: 2.7; Hatchfield+ 2021: 0.8). Sgr A* gets about a millionth of that at its Bondi radius (Baganoff+ 2003, Chandra: ~1e-6 Msun/yr of hot gas), and near the event horizon Faraday rotation limits it to 2e-9 - 2e-7 (Marrone+ 2007, with the SMA). Eight orders of magnitude. Simulations fill in the middle -- magnetic transport from the ring is at most 0.01-0.1 (Tress+ 2024, an upper limit), stellar feedback drives 5e-3 at 100 pc down to 1e-6 at 1 pc, with bursts of 1e-3 at 10 pc lasting 3-5 Myr (Feng+ 2026). So: does gas get there at all, and how? Radii for the MRI limit (~100 pc, the ring) and the horizon point (~10 R_s) are approximate placements.

We know the solar neighborhood in 3D, but not the GC

Molinari+ 2011: the "100-pc ring", face-on

We have beautiful edge-on, two-dimensional maps of the Galactic Center, but really poor three-dimensional maps. And that has resulted in a lot of discussion and controversy.

One hypothesis is that the molecular gas is predominantly constrained to a ring. This is known as the Molinari ring. This was hinted at by Herschel data. And it's the simplest view, simplest geometry. It doesn't hold up in a lot of ways, but it's simple and captures several bulk features well.

Nested spiral arms have been around as an idea for 30-50 years and still are reasonable models.

The inner ring could instead be an inner disk, roughly uniformly filled in.

But the reality is almost certainly the most complex option, that it's a mix of everything. The simulations hint at this. They also tell us that, depending on when we are viewing the Galactic Center, any one of these features could be dominant.

How are we going to distinguish these different scenarios? We have to measure the line-of-sight depth.

This idea comes from Arianna Vasini, who is a postdoc working with Mattia Sormani: as long as we can measure the stellar orbital motion, we can figure out the position of stars near the Galactic Center. Focus on this plot in the bottom right for just a moment. The proper motion of stars on typical orbits slowly increases from zero kiloparsecs, next to the Sun, up to eight kiloparsecs. And then, right at that eight-kiloparsec point, there is a sharp turnaround, where, in the central hundred parsecs, the proper motion goes from left to right. That's shown in the animated diagram. All the stars we see are moving slowly on the sky. Those in front are moving left, those behind are moving right. If a cloud sits in the Galactic Center, it preferentially blocks the stars moving right, and not block the ones that are in front, moving left. By counting the relative numbers of those, and ideally measuring the extinction toward ones in front of and behind a given cloud, we're able to do the same kind of tomography that was used in local clouds to make that beautiful top-down view that I opened with. From Arianna's modeling work, the prediction is that with Roman, we should have enough stars to get roughly 10-parsec line-of-sight resolution, 10-parsec three-dimensional resolution. We can already start to do some proper motion work with just JWST, but Roman is really going to open it up.

The envelope completely absorbs it. But there are outflow cavities, and some of the light either escapes through the outflow cavity, or escapes into the cavity and is scattered into the line of sight through the optically thinner zones.

The short version is: ALMA and Webb are seeing different parts of young stellar objects. And by selection, then, ALMA is seeing the younger and gas-richer sources, which Webb generally misses.

Star formation in the CMZ does not follow the local relation

So the story is that star formation in the Central Molecular Zone doesn't follow the local relation. It is underproducing stars given the amount of dense gas.

  • Nearby clouds all follow one relation, Sigma_SFR ~ Sigma_gas^2.0, with a uniform efficiency per free-fall time (eps_ff ~ 0.026)
  • Say the caveat out loud: Pokhrel+ fitted this only below 676 Msun/pc^2 (their N(H2) < 3e22 cut), and their own Fig. 3 shows eps_ff declining above ~1000. Every CMZ point here is 1–2 dex beyond the calibration — pure extrapolation, exactly as I said about Gutermuth in the 2018 paper
  • The bands are cloud-to-cloud scatter: 0.30 dex, so 2 sigma = 0.60 dex

Where each CMZ point comes from — one cloud-averaged point each, all at t_YSO = 0.1 Myr, and the two limits drawn as generously as the data allow, so the gap is not overstated:

  • Why 0.1 Myr: it is the Stage 0 (youngest protostar) lifetime, and the short age is the generous choice here — Sigma_SFR = Sigma_* / age, so a shorter age raises every CMZ rate toward the relation. The same age is used for all three points.
  • Sgr B2 (Ginsburg+ 2018), cloud-averaged: (log Sigma_gas, log Sigma_SFR) = (3.82, 3.19) — ~0.35 dex below the relation (0.23–0.48 across the paper's Total–Totalmax range), inside the 2 sigma band. 1.5e6 Msun over 15 x 15 pc (225 pc^2) → 6.7e3 Msun/pc^2; SFR 0.035–0.062 Msun/yr (Table 2), computed there with a 0.74 Myr age, rescaled by 0.74 / 0.1 to the same 0.1 Myr. So at 0.1 Myr Sgr B2 as a whole is consistent with the local relation; the Brick and Cloud E/F are not. Caveats to own: the YSO masses assume 95 Msun per source (21.8 Msun mean x 23% IMF sampling), and the sample misses Class II sources, so the stellar mass may be underestimated by up to 2x.
  • The Brick (Walker+ 2021), log Sigma_gas = 3.71, limit log Sigma_SFR < 1.72 — ~1.6 dex below the relation, and ~1.5 dex below Sgr B2 at nearly the same gas surface density. This is from Matt Hosek's work, showing the range of IMF slopes measured in different clusters. Focus on the high-mass end: the normal Salpeter IMF is this black dashed line on top, and most regions in the galaxy fall up there. But in the Arches cluster specifically, the slope is shallower. The same is true in a handful of other massive clusters. In the top right, I show that normal IMF as we were showing before. And then the bottom right, we show what a top-heavy one would look like. And notably, the critical number here is two. For steeper than two, most of the mass is in low-mass stars. For shallower than two, most of the mass is in high-mass stars. It is possible that the Arches and Quintuplet and these other similar clusters are actually mass-dominated by high-mass stars.

In the work that made this original measurement, HST data were limited to 1.8 solar masses on the low end.

We expect to be able to go to 0.4 solar masses with Webb and constrain the peak of the IMF. That's future work that JWST has enabled.

There's more ice in these clouds than we see locally. The local clouds, from the Chamaeleon region very nearby the Sun, are black data points These come from spectra; you should trust them much more than you trust our measurements, which come from photometry.
