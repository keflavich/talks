The GC is special on the sky but cosmically commonplace
Why the Galactic Center matters beyond the Milky Way -- most stars in the universe formed at cosmic noon, z ~ 1-3 (half before z = 1.3), and they formed in denser gas, in more compact regions -- like the CMZ (Kruijssen & Longmore 2013: same gas surface densities, 1e2-1e3 Msun/pc^2, Mach numbers and gas-to-star ratios). Caveats they list themselves: the CMZ is more metal-rich, high-z galaxies have far more radiation and cosmic rays, and the CMZ forms stars 1-2 dex below the scaling relations. Click: the GC is special on the sky but cosmically commonplace.

Dozens of questions about the Galactic Center have been open for decades; here are 19, each dated by the paper that posed it (or made it pressing). The OB-star one is the obvious one after the local census (#topdown-local: ~50 O stars within 1.25 kpc of the Sun): nobody knows the CMZ's number. The HST Paschen-alpha survey (Wang+ 2010; Dong+ 2011, ~180 emission-line sources) and Mauerhan+ 2010 found the brightest, emission-line ones; Clark+ 2021 constrain the isolated population; the rest are behind A_V ~ 30 and unclassified -- the JWST GC Treasury colors (Cano-González+ 2021-style hot-star selection) are the way at it. The PeV one: H.E.S.S. 2016 found the CMZ's diffuse TeV emission traces the gas and needs protons up to ~1 PeV near Sgr A* -- but the photon map is gas × cosmic rays, and we only know the gas in projection (bonus: #bonus-he-state). The GeV excess (Goodenough & Hooper 2009, Fermi-LAT): extended ~GeV emission around the GC -- dark-matter annihilation or an unresolved millisecond-pulsar population, still argued.

Question 1: What planets form there? Open since 2016. Where we are: none known: 95% of known planets are within 3 kpc of the Sun. What comes next: Roman microlensing toward the bulge.

Almost every planet we know is within 3 kpc of the Sun

NASA Exoplanet Archive, Sept 2026
95% of all 6366 confirmed planets are inside 3 kpc; 82% inside 1 kpc. The little pile at 7-8 kpc is bulge microlensing, and it is nearly the whole of what we know about planets outside the solar neighborhood.

So the planet census and the star formation census are the SAME sample. Whatever is biased about one is biased about the other.

Same axis as the last slide, now logarithmic in the vertical: gray is the 6,366 confirmed planets we know today, 82% within 1 kpc.

Click: microlensing. Roman launched on August 30; its Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey is six ~70-day seasons, the first three and last three of the 5-year prime mission, so these are numbers for ~2031. About 1,000 bound planets, from a tenth of an Earth mass up to tens of Jupiters, at 0.3–30 au: Penny+ 2019 predicted 1,400, and the survey definition committee says the newer, lower measured event rates take that down by ~1.4x. (Free-floating planets, ~250 more, are not in this.) Their hosts: median 7.7 kpc, 90% between 6 and 10 kpc, because Roman's fields sit only ~1–1.5 degrees below the plane, so most of the lenses are bulge stars.

Click: transits. About 50,000 — Wilson+ 2023 with Kepler-like occurrence rates, and the ROTAC's estimate for the adopted design. If giant-planet occurrence tracks metallicity, as it does locally, it is 135,000–200,000. 90–95% are close-in giants, and most will stay candidates; a few thousand hot Jupiters can be confirmed from their secondary eclipses. Three quarters of them are between 5 and 10 kpc; the tail reaches ~20 kpc, the far side of the bulge.

So in five years the planet census stops being a solar-neighborhood census: of ~57,000 planets with distances, ~10% instead of 82% would be inside 1 kpc. (The local census keeps growing too — Gaia astrometry, PLATO — so "two-centered", not "moved".)

And Roman includes a Galactic Center field (Sgr A*, Arches, Quintuplet) at the same 12-minute cadence. For scale: the JWST GC Treasury (program 10678, catalog v1.9) has 3,747,256 sources in 68 NIRCam tiles, 0.20 deg2 of the CMZ, F212N and F480M, ~5,300 per arcmin2 detected and crowding-limited. The planet yields above are for the lower-extinction bulge fields, not the CMZ itself.

Planets need disks; do CMZ stars get them? Sgr C (Lu+ 2022, Nature Astronomy): ALMA long-baseline 1.3 mm resolves a Keplerian disk, radius ~2000 au, 4.7 Msun (factor ~2 uncertain) around a 31.7 +/- 4.7 Msun protostar, rotation v ~ r^-0.51 +/- 0.20 inside 1000 au, inclination 53 deg. Gravitationally stable, with two trailing spirals (A, B) best explained by a close flyby -- and a perturber with the right properties is identified. So an early O star here forms "in a similar manner" to low-mass stars: disk-mediated accretion and flyby-impacted disk evolution. Weng+ 2026 (ApJ 1003, 219; 300 au): the same source, disk + envelope + streamers, ~40 Msun from the dynamics (SED fits give ~20, Crowe+ 2025).

Zhang+ 2025 (ApJL 982, L10): 0.04" ALMA 1.3 mm of 20 km/s C1, C4 and Sgr C C4 -- 199 compact fragments, typical size ~370 au, the first sample of protostellar envelope/disk and prestellar-kernel candidates there.

Click: Sgr B2 (Budaiev+ 2024, ApJ 961, 4; Nazar's thesis work). 500 au (1 mm) and 700 au (3 mm): 371 sources at 3 mm, 222 at 1 mm; the 110 sources seen at 5000 au fragment into ~2 each. Only the four brightest Orion disks would be detectable at Sgr B2's distance, so if these are disks they are larger (>200 au) and more massive than any disk in the solar neighborhood. What gas reaches Sgr A*?

Sormani & Barnes 2019; Hatchfield+ 2021; Baganoff+ 2003; Marrone+ 2007; simulations: Tress+ 2024, Feng+ 2026
About a solar mass per year comes down the bar lanes into the CMZ (Sormani & Barnes 2019: 2.7; Hatchfield+ 2021: 0.8). Sgr A* gets about a millionth of that at its Bondi radius (Baganoff+ 2003, Chandra: ~1e-6 Msun/yr of hot gas), and near the event horizon Faraday rotation limits it to 2e-9 - 2e-7 (Marrone+ 2007, with the SMA). Eight orders of magnitude. Simulations fill in the middle -- magnetic transport from the ring is at most 0.01-0.1 (Tress+ 2024, an upper limit), stellar feedback drives 5e-3 at 100 pc down to 1e-6 at 1 pc, with bursts of 1e-3 at 10 pc lasting 3-5 Myr (Feng+ 2026). So: does gas get there at all, and how? Radii for the MRI limit (~100 pc, the ring) and the horizon point (~10 R_s) are approximate placements.

Tress+ 2024: the magneto-rotational instability (MRI) moves gas in from the ring
  • MHD of gas in a Milky Way bar potential (Arepo). No self-gravity, star formation or feedback
  • The bar fills the CMZ ring at ~1 M☉ yr−1, with or without a field
  • Inside the ring only the MHD run has inflow: magnetic (Maxwell) stresses carry angular momentum out and gas drifts in at 0.01–0.1 M☉ yr−1. Without a field it is ~zero: all the gas stays in the ring
  • Not converged: each 10× finer cell mass cuts the inflow ~5–10× (Fig. B1). The authors call 0.01–0.1 an upper limit
What 3D data can test here
  • Predicted, and observable: the ordered field follows the gas flow; B ≈ 100 μG (n/103 cm−3)0.33; a slow radial drift of gas inside the ring
  • Ṁ(R) = 2πR Σ(R) vR(R) needs face-on Σ and vR. Seen edge-on, the line-of-sight velocity mixes rotation and inflow; positions along the line of sight separate them

Star formation in the CMZ does not follow the local relation
  • Nearby clouds all follow one relation, Sigma_SFR ~ Sigma_gas^2.0, with a uniform efficiency per free-fall time (eps_ff ~ 0.026)
  • Say the caveat out loud: Pokhrel+ fitted this only below 676 Msun/pc^2 (their N(H2) < 3e22 cut), and their own Fig. 3 shows eps_ff declining above ~1000. Every CMZ point here is 1–2 dex beyond the calibration — pure extrapolation, exactly as I said about Gutermuth in the 2018 paper
  • The bands are cloud-to-cloud scatter: 0.30 dex, so 2 sigma = 0.60 dex

Where each CMZ point comes from — one cloud-averaged point each, all at t_YSO = 0.1 Myr, and the two limits drawn as generously as the data allow, so the gap is not overstated:

  • Why 0.1 Myr: it is the Stage 0 (youngest protostar) lifetime, and the short age is the generous choice here — Sigma_SFR = Sigma_* / age, so a shorter age raises every CMZ rate toward the relation. The same age is used for all three points.
  • Sgr B2 (Ginsburg+ 2018), cloud-averaged: (log Sigma_gas, log Sigma_SFR) = (3.82, 3.19) — ~0.35 dex below the relation (0.23–0.48 across the paper's Total–Totalmax range), inside the 2 sigma band. 1.5e6 Msun over 15 x 15 pc (225 pc^2) → 6.7e3 Msun/pc^2; SFR 0.035–0.062 Msun/yr (Table 2), computed there with a 0.74 Myr age, rescaled by 0.74 / 0.1 to the same 0.1 Myr. So at 0.1 Myr Sgr B2 as a whole is consistent with the local relation; the Brick and Cloud E/F are not. Caveats to own: the YSO masses assume 95 Msun per source (21.8 Msun mean x 23% IMF sampling), and the sample misses Class II sources, so the stellar mass may be underestimated by up to 2x.
  • The Brick (Walker+ 2021), log Sigma_gas = 3.71, limit log Sigma_SFR < 1.72 — ~1.6 dex below the relation, and ~1.5 dex below Sgr B2 at nearly the same gas surface density. Cloud-scale aperture, to match how Pokhrel measure and so the two limits are comparable: >1e5 Msun within R = 2–3 pc (Sec 1); R = 2.5 pc → 19.6 pc^2 → 5.1e3 Msun/pc^2. Walker+ quote no SFR and no limit, so the ceiling is ours and deliberately generous: every Table 3 core at its 22 K mass, source 1 counted whole (64.2 Msun) not split, = 104 Msun of core gas, times a core-to-star efficiency of 1.0 — every gram already a star — / 0.1 Myr / 19.6 pc^2 → 53 Msun/pc^2/Myr. Cross-check: one 5 Msun star (the measured core) extrapolated over a Kroupa IMF to 150 Msun is 117 Msun, within ~10% of the core sum. These cores really are protostellar: 9 of 18 drive SiO outflows. Using the 19" ALMA field alone instead (7e3 Msun, 1.75 pc^2) moves it to (4.0e3, 594) — same cloud, aperture ten times smaller, and then only ~0.3 dex below.
  • Cloud E/F (Barnes+ 2019 + Walker+ 2018), log Sigma_gas = 3.78, limit log Sigma_SFR < 1.98 — ~1.5 dex below the relation, ~1.2 dex below Sgr B2. Sigma_gas from Walker+ 2018 Table 1, which tabulates the dust ridge on a consistent footing: cloud e = 11.2e4 Msun in R = 2.4 pc, cloud f = 7.3e4 Msun in R = 2.0 pc; Barnes treat them as one cloud, so 1.85e5 Msun over 30.7 pc^2 = 6.0e3 Msun/pc^2. The Immer+ 2012 column of the same table gives 2.6e3, so the horizontal position is good to a factor of ~2. The ceiling is set by what is observed: no HII regions, no protostellar source catalog, and the only star formation signature in either cloud is one H2O + Class II CH3OH maser pair in the south. A Class II methanol maser marks a high-mass protostar, so allow one star of ≥ 10 Msun and the full Kroupa IMF that comes with it (to 150 Msun): 295 Msun. / 0.1 Myr / 30.7 pc^2 → 96 Msun/pc^2/Myr. (This is now above the Brick's ceiling, because of the full-IMF extrapolation.)
  • Not drawn any more (single points since 2026-10-19), in case it comes up: the range of column density each cloud actually covers: from the CMZoom threshold N(H2) = 1e23 cm^-2 (Walker+ 2018 Sec 2.1, "designed to target all regions within the CMZ that lie above a column density threshold of ~1e23 cm^-2") = 2.2e3 Msun/pc^2, up to the densest structure in each cloud — the Brick's maser core, 72 Msun in R = 0.04 pc (Rathborne+ 2015 via Walker+ 2021) = 1.4e4 = 6.4e23 cm^-2; and Cloud E/F's peak column, 3.7e24 cm^-2 (Barnes+ 2019 Sec 3.1) = 8.3e4. Drawn as flat bars, at the cloud-averaged limit, they would be the conservative rendering: a contour-by-contour treatment tilts them upward to the right, since the area shrinks at higher contour — that is why Pokhrel's clouds are curves, not points.
  • Cross-check on which of the two is denser (Tang+ 2021, MNRAS 505, 2392, Fig. 10): their AzTEC+Herschel dust-SED fit gives one uniform N(H2) map of the whole CMZ at 7", so the Brick and Cloud E/F are measured the same way. Reading that map in matched R = 2.4 pc apertures: mean lg N(H2) = 22.90 for the Brick, 23.03 for cloud e and 23.01 for cloud f. Cloud E/F is the denser of the two, by ~0.13 dex (~40%). That is the same ordering the Walker+ 2018 masses give (5.1e3 vs 6.0e3 Msun/pc^2, 0.07 dex), and by a slightly larger margin — so the separation plotted here is the conservative one. Both clouds saturate Tang's 10^23.4 color scale over a few per cent of their area, so these are means, not peaks.
  • Two things I got wrong first, in case they come up: (i) I first took Sigma_gas from Barnes' intro prose, "~1e5 Msun, radii of ~1 pc". Multiplied together those give 3.2e4 Msun/pc^2, five to thirteen times too high, and put E/F to the right of most of Sgr B2. They are order-of-magnitude statements; Barnes never tabulate a cloud-scale mass or radius. Walker+ 2018 Table 1 is the number to use. (ii) I first set the E/F ceiling from Barnes' "the core region could be up to ~600 Msun ... capable of forming one or several high-mass stars (~25 per cent efficiency)". That 150 Msun is star formation potential, not stars that exist — those cores are starless — and treating it as already formed put the limit inside the Sgr B2 cloud.

The MUBLO: 160 km s−1 lines and no known counterpart

Ginsburg+ 2024 (discovery) · ALMA images: Ginsburg+ in prep · bottom row: JWST NIRCam 2.12 / 4.8 μm and ALMA CS 2–1
Top row: new ALMA images at 0.07-0.09" (11-14x finer than the discovery data): 3 mm, 850 um and 450 um continuum show a compact core plus an arc offset by 0.33" (~2700 au), seen in all three bands; SO 3(2)-2(1) is the line emission (bigger beam). Bottom row: JWST NIRCam 2.12 um and 4.8 um, a 4.8/2.12 composite, and CS 2-1. At 4.8 um the MUBLO is a dark patch against the diffuse background -- it is in front of the Galactic-center starlight, so it absorbs. There is a 4.8 um point source near the crosshair; whether it has anything to do with the MUBLO is NOT established -- the line is "no counterpart identified so far".

The magenta crosshair in each panel marks the MUBLO's catalog position (RA 266.4905821, Dec -28.9529931, ICRS; the discovery position from Ginsburg+ 2024), which is also the center of every cutout -- not a fitted continuum peak, and not any near-IR candidate. The core + arc structure means the brightest pixel need not sit on it.

The Millimeter Ultra-Broad-Line Object, G0.02467−0.0727, found in ACES 3 mm data (Ginsburg+ 2024, ApJL 968, L11). Published: continuum spectral index ~3.3 (dust); CS, SO and SO2 lines with FWHM ~160 km/s and a Gaussian-looking profile; emitting area <~1" (<~10^4 au); v_LSR ~40-50 km/s, so it is in the Galactic center; LTE temperature from several SO lines 13 K (low-density, subthermally excited warmer gas not excluded); no SiO, so no strong (>~10 km/s) shocks; no detection at X-ray, infrared or radio wavelengths. The paper weighed eight kinds of explanation; most are inconsistent with the data or do not fully explain them -- it called the MUBLO "observationally unique".

Top: new ALMA images at 0.07-0.09" (11-14x finer than the discovery data): 3 mm, 850 um and 450 um continuum show a compact core plus an arc offset by 0.33" (~2700 au), seen in all three bands; SO is the line emission (bigger beam). Lower left: the SED -- a dust-like millimeter rise (ALMA points, with modified blackbodies at 10-25 K for scale) and only upper limits (Spitzer, ISO backgrounds) everywhere else. The mm spectral index of the core alone is ~2.4 and the continuum fluxes are PRELIMINARY -- I have not put them on the slide. Excitation temperature ~13-14 K.

Chemistry: seven transitions of four hydrogen-free sulfur species are detected (E_u = 7-88 K), while eleven hydrogen-bearing species have only upper limits; N(HCN)/N(CS) is ~22-28x below the surrounding cloud. It looks hydrogen-poor, which no standard picture (protostellar outflow, evolved star, ISM feature) accommodates comfortably.

No counterpart has been identified at any wavelength shorter than ~1 mm, so we do not know what powers it. The JWST Cycle 6 proposal (submitted, not awarded) asks for spectroscopy: hydrogen recombination lines (Br-alpha) and a near-IR counterpart would separate a stellar/accretion origin from a hydrogen-depleted ejection. 1" = 8200 au at 8.2 kpc. Do not quote a mass.

left: MIRI F2550W / F1280W / F770W (R/G/B)  ·  right: F2550W alone, the boxed region
  • Patchy, flocculent emission ~30″ (~1.2 pc) across, roughly circular, starting at the edge of Sgr B2 DS

Budaiev+ 2026, AJ 172, 51, Fig. 9
From Budaiev+ 2026, "Extended flocculence in Sgr B2 DS": extended 25 um emission in part of Sgr B2 DS, flocculent, with identifiable local peaks and missing patches. Parts of it are visible in F1280W, F770W and Br-alpha, but extremely faint and indistinguishable from other large-scale features; the structure is only apparent at 25 um (their Fig. 9 caption). Not detected by SOFIA at 25 and 37 um or Spitzer at 24 um, likely because of sensitivity and resolution. It begins near the edge of Sgr B2 DS and extends for 30" in a circular shape (30" = 1.2 pc at their 8.277 kpc; the panel's scale bar is 5" = 0.2 pc). The top side is dimmer -- either no emission there, or foreground material extincting it. In their words: "We do not know what drives this unique morphology"; the rough symmetry hints at a central feedback-driven mechanism, "there is no obvious configuration that would produce such a flocculent morphology", and the source of the emission is unclear.

The dashed gray circles in the right panel are sources affected by F2550W persistence (their appendix), not features. The third panel of the paper figure (not shown here) is the same field in Br-alpha: filaments and stars, not the same patches.

Not to be confused with the other 25 um result in the same paper: around Sgr B2 N, 25 um emission follows the large-scale SiO outflow, which they read as infrared radiation escaping the protocluster along the outflow cavities. That one has an explanation; this one does not. The same figure's left panel also shows the sharp eastern edge of Sgr B2 DS.

State of the art, 2024-2026 (all checked in the arXiv sources):
- H.E.S.S. 2026 (Acharyya+ 2026, A&A accepted, arXiv:2608.25946 -- the refereed version of Devin+ 2025; ~365 h, 2004-2020): a 3D likelihood fit (l, b, E). The gas is the Tsuboi+ 1999 CS(1-0) cube, each velocity band assigned the line-of-sight distance from Sawada+ 2004's face-on map -- assuming CS and CO are distributed alike, with "the limited line-of-sight (z axis) resolution of 30 pc" of Sawada's map. A 1/r^alpha cosmic-ray profile times that gas gives alpha = 1.10 +- 0.05 stat +- 0.10 syst (MAGIC 2020: 1.2 +- 0.3; H.E.S.S. 2016: 1.10 +- 0.12): the ridge "is well reproduced with the assumed gas distribution model".
- New in the refereed paper: (a) the ridge spectrum curves (>3 sigma) -- parent protons break at ~15-50 TeV (or cut off at 60-160 TeV), so "PeVatron" needs care; HAWC's emission to 100 TeV favors curvature over a cutoff. (b) They concede that for Sgr B2 and the 20/50 km/s clouds "the 30 pc resolution of Sawada's model appears limiting and leads to unrealistic dispersion along the line of sight". (c) Run in reverse, assuming stationary injection at the GC: Sgr C closer than ~90 pc along the line of sight is disfavored, "thus putting the Twisted Ellipse and Open Streams models in tension"; the 20/50 km/s clouds act like compact clouds 20-35 and 40-55 pc from the GC (not >~100 pc, as in Sofue's two arms); the Dust Ridge within +-60 pc; Sgr B2 unconstrained (only a 2% flux change). They call these "qualitative" and close: CTAO "might be able to ... place stronger constraints on 3D CMZ models". Note the circularity: the distances come from the same 1/r fit the gas model was used to derive.
- HAWC 2024 (ApJL 973, L34): the PeVatron confirmed beyond 100 TeV; gas from the same CS map (5e6 Msun in the H.E.S.S. regions).
- Ren+ 2025 (A&A 697, A42; MPIK): a new CO + dust (Herschel, Planck) column and X_CO analysis for the CMZ -- and the quote: the lack of a reliable total gas column density model is still the limiting factor.
- Models: Dorner+ 2024 (ApJ 965, 180; 3D anisotropic transport) use Ferriere+ 2007 -- with its scale height raised from 18 to 30 pc to match the gamma-ray thickness -- and note the "lack of substructures within the gas distribution"; Becker Tjus+ 2022 (CRPropa) add discrete clouds (Sgr B2, the dust ridge) and a 3D field to Ferriere; Scherer+ 2022-2024 use disk or ring CMZ toys traced by CO; Lai+ 2026 (PRD) propose neutrinos to test the gas tracers, noting CS and dust disagree significantly. CTAO's LST-1 has begun GC observations (Abe+ 2025).
- The 2018 H.E.S.S. line is still worth quoting: "Since there is no reliable 3D model of the gas distribution in the CMZ, we do not model the ridge emission with a physically motivated CR density."
So the gas side is a 1999 CS cube plus a 2004 face-on map -- before the orbit models, ACES, or JWST.

A power law fit at projected distance, to the five clouds that every geometry places (MC50, MC20, the Bridge, Sgr C, Sgr B2; all three tracers, with the tracer spread folded into the errors), gives alpha = 0.84 +- 0.08, chi2 3.1/3. That is shallower than their 1.10 from the full 3D fit, as it should be if the inner clouds are farther from Sgr A* than they look.

The method exists, and it works on mock data
The nuclear stellar disk rotates: near side toward positive longitude, far side toward negative — a ~5 mas/yr difference against ~1 mas/yr scatter. A proper motion therefore places a star on the near or the far side, and its extinction measures the dust in front of it.

The 3D shape and orbits of the CMZ. What is constrained: the dense gas spans 1.8 > l > -1.3 deg with 70-75% at positive longitude (3D CMZ I), and every independent near/far tracer so far (H2CO absorption in III, 8/70 micron extinction in IV, star counts + X-ray echoes + proper motions combined in V, stellar proper motions in Nogueras-Lara+26) puts most clouds on the near side: 78% of the 23 sources placed by absorption. What is not: the orbit. The KDL open stream scores best in both III (74% near/far accuracy, table) and IV (65% min-distance agreement), but the toy ellipse is close (55-67%), and V finds that no single x2 orbit fits; it prefers nested orbits with semi-major axes ~72-146 pc. The 20 and 50 km/s clouds are the sharpest disagreement: stellar proper motions give 43+-8 and 56+-11 pc (lower limits); 3D CMZ V gives ~31 and ~42 pc (but uses the Nogueras-Lara distances as a prior, so not independent); Mills+26 argue >10 pc, likely 15-50 pc; older models (interaction with Sgr A East and the CND) put them within ~5-20 pc; Sofue+25a puts the 20 km/s cloud on Arm III (42 pc in front) and the 50 km/s cloud either near Sgr A or on Arm V (~8 pc). Sofue's ACES papers are interpretive models of l-v data (nested arms/rings), and Sofue+25a puts Sgr C on the far-side Arm II while 3D CMZ IV and V place Sgr C on the near side. Proper motions are the direct test: Henshaw+16 proposed maser proper motions; Nogueras-Lara+26 is the first stellar proper-motion placement and calls itself a pilot for the whole CMZ, which is where Roman comes in. Kruijssen+19 and Tress+24 are simulations; Anderson+20 interprets Sgr E with simulations.

ALMA Central Molecular Zone Exploration Survey (ACES) I: Overview. Star formation theory is calibrated on a small, nearby, unrepresentative sample

  • Everything we have resolved is within ~1 kpc. So is almost every planet we know.
  • That volume has ~15-20 high-mass star-forming regions and no young massive clusters — yet clusters like that were a major mode of star formation in the early Universe.
  • ALMA and JWST have just made disk-scale imaging and YSO demographics possible everywhere else in the Galaxy.
  • The Galactic Center is the best stress test we have — and it is already showing stress.
